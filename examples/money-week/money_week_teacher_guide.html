Money Week — Teacher / Parent Guide

Theme: Money  |  Audience: Christopher, age 6, K–1  |  Narrator: Penny the Piggy Bank

Subjects: Math (VA.MATH.K.k.7, VA.MATH.1.1.8, VA.MATH.K.k.6, VA.MATH.1.1.6) + Social Studies (VA.HISTORY.K.k.8, VA.HISTORY.1.1.8)

Causal Arc: What is money → Coins & their values → Counting coins → Earning & saving → Needs vs. Wants & smart choices

Monday — What Is Money?

Standards: VA.HISTORY.K.k.8, VA.HISTORY.1.1.8

Answer Key — Reading Questions

Q1 (Why money was invented): People invented money to solve the problems of bartering. With bartering, both people had to want what the other person had at the same time. Money is something everyone agrees has value, so you can trade it for anything.

Q2 (Goods vs. services): A good is something you can hold and take home (physical object). A service is something someone does for you. Examples of goods: toy, apple, book. Examples of services: haircut, doctor visit, piano lesson. Accept any reasonable examples.

Q3 (Bartering): Bartering is trading without money. Personal response — accept any real-life trading example (e.g., swapping snacks, sharing toys).

T-Chart Answer Key

Goods: Apple, Library book, Soccer ball, Toy car, Backpack

Services: Haircut, Doctor visit, Pizza delivery, Piano lesson, Lawn mowing

Note: "Pizza delivery" could spark discussion — the pizza itself is a good, but the delivery is a service. Accept either column for the delivery item if the child can explain their reasoning.

LET'S DISCUSS Guidance

"If you had apples and needed shoes, how would bartering work? What problems might come up?"

Key insight: The shoemaker must ALSO want apples right now (double coincidence of wants). What if apples are plentiful but the shoemaker wants meat? This is why money — a universally accepted medium of exchange — was such an important invention. Encourage the child to imagine several barter scenarios to feel the friction.

Misconceptions to Watch

Children often think money is "just paper" or coins with no intrinsic value, or conversely that gold and silver have some magical value. The key concept: money works because everyone agrees it works — it is a social technology built on trust and shared agreement.

Extension Activity

Barter game: Give the child five small objects (a crayon, a sticker, a block, etc.). Give a parent/sibling five different objects. Try to "barter" to get something you want. Talk about how hard it is to find an exact match. Then introduce a "pretend money" token (poker chip or button) and see how much easier trading becomes.

Tuesday — Coins and Their Values

Standards: VA.MATH.K.k.7

Answer Key — Reading Questions

Q1 (Coins least to greatest): Penny (1¢), Nickel (5¢), Dime (10¢), Quarter (25¢).

Q2 (Equivalencies): 5 pennies = 1 nickel. 10 pennies = 1 dime.

Q3 (Size vs. value): No — size does NOT tell us the value of a coin. The dime is the smallest coin but is worth 10¢, more than the penny (1¢) or nickel (5¢). The appearance and value of a coin are set by the government, not by size.

Feature Matrix Answer Key

Penny: Copper color ✓, Worth less than 10¢ ✓, Has a president on the front ✓

Nickel: Silver color ✓, Worth less than 10¢ ✓, Has a president on the front ✓, Worth exactly 5 pennies ✓

Dime: Silver color ✓, Worth 10¢ or more ✓, Smallest coin by size ✓, Has a president on the front ✓

Quarter: Silver color ✓, Worth 10¢ or more ✓, Has a president on the front ✓, Takes 4 to make a dollar ✓

Note on "Has a president on the front": All four coins feature a historical president or leader. Roosevelt on the dime is technically a president. All four should be checked for this property.

LET'S DISCUSS Guidance

"Why do coins have pictures of famous people? Who would YOU put on a coin?"

Coins honor people considered important to a nation's history or values. The people on US coins were chosen by Congress. There is no single right answer for who the child would choose — the goal is creative reasoning and discussion about what makes a person worth honoring. Ask: what did that person do? Why does it matter?

Misconceptions to Watch

The "big coin = more money" confusion is extremely common at age 6. Use physical coins to demonstrate repeatedly: hold a dime and a nickel side by side. The nickel looks bigger but the dime is worth more. Reinforce: the government decides value — not the size or weight of the metal.

Extension Activity

Coin rubbings: Place real coins under a piece of paper and rub a crayon over them to reveal the images. Label each rubbing with the coin's name and value. Then sort the rubbings from least to greatest value and tape them in order on a strip of paper to make a "value line."

Wednesday — Counting Coins

Standards: VA.MATH.1.1.8, VA.MATH.K.k.6

Answer Key — Reading Questions

Q1 (5 nickels): Count by 5s: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25. Total = 25¢.

Q2 (8 dimes): Count by 10s: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80. Total = 80¢.

Q3 (4 quarters): Count by 25s: 25, 50, 75, 100. Total = 100¢ = $1.00 (one dollar).

Q4 (9 pennies vs. 2 nickels): 9 pennies = 9¢. 2 nickels = 10¢. The friend with 2 nickels has more money (10¢ > 9¢). Key insight: more coins does not always mean more money.

Cause-and-Effect Answer Key

6 pennies: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 → Total = 6¢

3 nickels: 5, 10, 15 → Total = 15¢

7 dimes: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70 → Total = 70¢

2 quarters: 25, 50 → Total = 50¢

10 dimes: 10 × 10 = 100¢ = $1.00. Yes, exactly enough for a $1.00 snack.

LET'S DISCUSS Guidance

"Why start with the highest-value coin? What if you counted pennies first?"

Starting high keeps the numbers manageable and reduces error. If you count pennies first in a mixed pile, you might lose track of which coins you have counted. More importantly, this builds the habit needed for later multi-coin counting. Connect to real-world cashier behavior: cashiers always handle large bills/coins first.

Misconceptions to Watch

"More coins = more money" — nine pennies feel like more than two nickels, but 9¢ < 10¢. Use physical coins whenever possible. Have the child count each pile and write the total before comparing. Let the numbers do the talking.

Extension Activity

Piggy bank challenge: Put a random pile of only one coin type (e.g., all nickels) in front of the child — anywhere from 3 to 12 coins. Set a 30-second timer and see if they can skip-count to the total before time runs out. Gradually increase the number of coins as fluency grows.

Thursday — Earning and Saving Money

Standards: VA.HISTORY.K.k.8, VA.MATH.1.1.6

Answer Key — Reading Questions

Q1 (Earning): Earning money means doing a job or chore and receiving money as payment. Examples for a child: folding laundry, sweeping the floor, watering plants, feeding a pet, setting the table. Accept any reasonable home-chore examples.

Q2 (Spending vs. saving): Spending is using money to buy something right now. Saving is keeping money to use later (for something bigger). Saving fills up Penny the Piggy Bank.

Q3 (5 pennies × 6 days): 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30 → 30¢ after 6 days.

Q4 (12¢ + 8¢): 12 + 8 = 20¢.

Matching Answer Key

Earning → Doing a job and receiving money as payment

Saving → Keeping money to use in the future

Spending → Using money to buy something now

Income → The money you receive from doing a job

Bartering → Trading one thing for another without money

Goods → Things you can hold and take home that you buy

LET'S DISCUSS Guidance

"Would you spend 20 cents on a small treat now, or save it for something bigger?"

There is no wrong answer — this is about the child's values and future-thinking skills. If they say spend: ask what they would buy and why. If they say save: ask what they are saving for and how long it will take. The goal is deliberate decision-making, not a morality lesson about spending being bad.

Misconceptions to Watch

Children may think "saving" means hiding money somewhere or that it is only for big purchases. Clarify: saving is any intentional decision to keep money for later use, even if it is just for next week. Also reinforce: money in a piggy bank is still yours — it has not been "used up."

Extension Activity

Savings goal chart: Help the child pick one item they want to save for (even something small, like a 50¢ sticker pack). Draw a simple bar or thermometer chart with the goal amount at the top. Each time they earn coins, color in the amount earned. This makes abstract saving visible and motivating.

Friday — Needs vs. Wants (Capstone)

Standards: VA.HISTORY.1.1.8, VA.MATH.1.1.8

Answer Key — Reading Questions

Q1 (Needs vs. wants): A need is something required for survival and health (food, water, clothing, shelter, healthcare). A want is something desirable but not essential (toys, candy, fancy things). Examples: Needs = vegetables, a coat; Wants = video game, candy bar. Accept reasonable examples.

Q2 (Trade-off): A trade-off is giving up one thing to get another because you cannot have both. Money example: If you spend 50¢ on a toy, you cannot also spend that 50¢ on a snack — you had to choose one and give up the other.

Q3 (75¢ − 25¢ − 50¢): 75 − 25 = 50, then 50 − 50 = 0. Christopher has 0¢ left.

Q4 (Needs before wants): Money is limited. If you spend on wants first, you might not have enough left for things you truly need to stay healthy and safe. Families must prioritize to make sure essential items are covered before extras.

Q5 (Favorite lesson): Personal response — accept any well-reasoned answer.

Word Sort Answer Key

Needs: Vegetables, Clean water, Winter coat, Doctor visit, A safe home, Bread

Wants: Video game, Stuffed animal, Candy bar, Fancy shoes, New bicycle, Movie ticket

Note: "Fancy shoes" are a WANT (basic shoes are a need, but fancy/expensive ones are a want). "New bicycle" could spark discussion — basic transportation might be a need in some situations. Accept either column for bicycle if the child can explain their reasoning. "Stuffed animal" may feel like a need for emotional comfort — acknowledge the feeling and guide toward the distinction between physical necessity and emotional comfort.

LET'S DISCUSS Guidance

"If your family had exactly $10 left this week, what would you spend it on?"

This is the week's capstone reasoning exercise. Guide: start with needs first (food, any essential item). Then see if wants fit in the remaining budget. Key insight: the order matters — needs first, then wants with what is left. Discuss: who in the family has input in this decision? How do different family members' needs get balanced? This is a real-world budgeting simulation.

Misconceptions to Watch

"Wants are bad." Correct this gently — wants are not bad! Enjoying nice things is perfectly fine when needs are covered and money permits. The lesson is about sequence and priority, not about denying enjoyment. A healthy relationship with money includes room for wants.

Extension Activity

Grocery store needs/wants hunt: On the next grocery trip, give the child a small notebook. As items go into the cart, they write "N" for need or "W" for want next to each one. After shopping, count the Ns and Ws. What percentage of the cart was needs? Talk about why families might also include a few wants (treats, special items) in a grocery run.

Week Summary — Causal Chain

The week followed this chain of concepts:

  1. Monday: Bartering problems → money invented as a universal medium of exchange; goods vs. services distinction.
  2. Tuesday: The four US coins and their values; size does not equal value; key equivalencies.
  3. Wednesday: Counting collections of like coins using skip-counting; more coins ≠ more money.
  4. Thursday: Earning money through work; saving vs. spending; single-step addition money problems.
  5. Friday: Needs vs. wants; trade-offs; applying all week's concepts to a real budget scenario.

By Friday, Christopher should be able to identify all four coins and their values, count a pile of like coins, explain the difference between earning and saving, and correctly sort everyday items into needs vs. wants — and explain why the order matters when money is limited.
