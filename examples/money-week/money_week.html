Meet Penny — and Why Money Matters!

Meet Penny the Piggy Bank! Penny is a shiny pink piggy bank with a big smile and a slot on her back just wide enough for a coin. She sits on Christopher's bookshelf and every day she says, 'Put a little money in me and watch it grow!'

But what exactly IS money? Money is anything that people agree to use to buy and sell things. Long ago, people traded things they had for things they needed — a farmer might trade a bag of apples for a pair of shoes. That is called bartering. But bartering was tricky! What if the shoemaker did not want apples that day? People needed something that everyone agreed was valuable — so coins and paper bills were invented.

Today we use money to buy goods and services. A good is something you can hold and take home — like a toy, a book, an apple, or a pair of shoes. A service is something someone does for you — like a haircut, a doctor's visit, or a teacher teaching a lesson. When you pay for a good or service, you give money, and the other person gives you what you asked for.

'Every coin in my belly once helped someone buy something important,' Penny oinked happily. 'Money makes trading much easier for everyone!'

Questions

1. Why did people invent money? What problem did it solve?
2. What is the difference between a good and a service? Give one example of each.
3. What is bartering? Can you think of a time you traded something with a friend?
4. LET'S DISCUSS: If you had a bag of apples and needed new shoes, how would you use bartering to get them? What problems might come up?

Words to Know

money: Coins and bills that people agree to use to buy and sell things.
bartering: Trading one thing for another without using money — like swapping apples for shoes.
goods: Things you can hold and take home that you buy with money, like food, toys, or clothes.
services: Things people do for you that you pay for, like a haircut or a doctor's visit.
trade: Giving something to get something else in return — buying is a kind of trade.

Penny Introduces Her Coin Friends

Penny shook herself and four coins tumbled out onto the table. 'Let me introduce you to my four best friends!' she said proudly.

'First, meet the PENNY. A penny is a small copper-colored coin worth ONE cent. That means it takes 100 pennies to make one dollar! On the front is Abraham Lincoln, the 16th president of the United States. On the back is the Lincoln Memorial. Pennies are worth 1 cent — we write that as 1¢.'

'Next is the NICKEL. A nickel is a bigger, silver-colored coin worth FIVE cents. One nickel equals five pennies — you would need five pennies to match one nickel! Thomas Jefferson is on the front, and Monticello (his home) is on the back. '5¢.'

'Now meet the DIME. The dime is the SMALLEST coin, but do not let its size fool you — it is worth TEN cents! One dime equals two nickels, or ten pennies. Franklin Roosevelt is on the front, and a torch with an olive branch is on the back. '10¢.'

'Finally, the QUARTER. A quarter is a large, silver coin worth TWENTY-FIVE cents. It takes four quarters to make one dollar. George Washington is on the front. '25¢.'

Penny smiled. 'Now you know all four of my coin friends. Remember: penny = 1¢, nickel = 5¢, dime = 10¢, quarter = 25¢. Say it with me!'

Questions

1. List all four coins from least to greatest value. Write the name and the value (in cents) for each.
2. How many pennies equal one nickel? How many pennies equal one dime?
3. The dime is the smallest coin but it is worth more than a penny or a nickel. Does size tell us the value of a coin? Explain.
4. LET'S DISCUSS: Why do you think coins have pictures of famous people on them? What person would YOU put on a coin and why?

Words to Know

cent (¢): The smallest unit of money in the United States. 100 cents = 1 dollar.
penny: A copper-colored coin worth 1 cent. 100 pennies = $1.
nickel: A silver coin worth 5 cents. 5 pennies = 1 nickel.
dime: The smallest coin, worth 10 cents. 10 pennies or 2 nickels = 1 dime.
quarter: A large silver coin worth 25 cents. 4 quarters = $1 (one dollar).

How to Count a Pile of Coins

Penny had a very important lesson. 'Knowing what each coin is worth is great,' she said, 'but what about when you have a BUNCH of coins? How do you count them?'

The key is to start with the coin that is worth the MOST, and count down. But today, Penny wants to practice counting piles of the SAME coin — that is the first step before mixing coins together.

COUNTING PENNIES: Each penny is 1¢. Count them one by one: 1¢, 2¢, 3¢... If you have 7 pennies, you have 7¢. Easy!

COUNTING NICKELS: Each nickel is 5¢. Count by FIVES: 5¢, 10¢, 15¢, 20¢... If you have 4 nickels, count: 5, 10, 15, 20 — you have 20¢!

COUNTING DIMES: Each dime is 10¢. Count by TENS: 10¢, 20¢, 30¢, 40¢... If you have 6 dimes, count: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 — you have 60¢!

COUNTING QUARTERS: Each quarter is 25¢. Count by TWENTY-FIVES: 25¢, 50¢, 75¢, 100¢. 100¢ is the same as ONE DOLLAR, written as $1.00!

'The trick,' said Penny with a wink, 'is to know your skip-counting! Count by 1s for pennies, 5s for nickels, 10s for dimes, and 25s for quarters.' Christopher practiced counting and found he had exactly 35¢ in his nickel pile. 'Seven nickels!' he shouted. Penny rattled happily.

Questions

1. You have 5 nickels. Count by fives to find the total. How many cents is that?
2. You have 8 dimes. Count by tens to find the total. How many cents is that?
3. You have 4 quarters. Count by twenty-fives. How many cents is that? What is another word for that amount?
4. If you have 9 pennies and your friend has 2 nickels, who has more money? How do you know?
5. LET'S DISCUSS: Why is it smart to count the highest-value coins first when you have a mixed pile? What might happen if you counted the pennies first?

Words to Know

value: How much a coin is worth — the number of cents.
count by fives: Saying numbers in jumps of five: 5, 10, 15, 20 ... Used to count nickels.
count by tens: Saying numbers in jumps of ten: 10, 20, 30, 40 ... Used to count dimes.
dollar ($1.00): One hundred cents. Four quarters, ten dimes, twenty nickels, or 100 pennies all equal one dollar.
total: The final amount you get when you add all the coins together.

How Does Money Get Into Penny?

Penny sighed happily as Christopher dropped three coins into her slot — clink, clink, CLINK! 'Where did you get those?' she asked. 'I helped my mom fold laundry,' said Christopher proudly. 'She gave me 15 cents!' Penny grinned. 'That is EARNING!'

Earning money means doing a job or chore and receiving money as payment. People earn money in many ways — parents go to work, kids do chores, and sometimes people sell things they have made or grown. The money you earn is called your income.

But what do you DO with money once you earn it? You have two choices: spend it now, or save it for later. Saving means keeping money so you can use it in the future — usually for something bigger. When you put money in a piggy bank, that is saving!

'I love it when people save,' said Penny. 'A little bit every day adds up fast. If you save just 5 pennies a day, after 10 days you will have 50 pennies — that is 50 cents! After 20 days, 100 pennies — that is a whole dollar!'

Christopher added up his savings in his head. He had 12 cents on Monday, earned 15 cents on Thursday, and was going to earn 10 more cents this weekend. 'How much will I have total?' he asked. Penny rattled with excitement. 'Let us figure it out together: 12 + 15 = 27, and 27 + 10 = 37. Thirty-seven cents!'

Questions

1. What does it mean to earn money? Give two examples of ways a child can earn money.
2. What is the difference between spending and saving? Which one fills up Penny?
3. Christopher saves 5 pennies every day. How much will he have after 6 days? Show your counting.
4. If Christopher has 12 cents and earns 8 more cents for setting the table, how much money does he have now?
5. LET'S DISCUSS: If you had 20 cents, would you spend it right away on a small treat, or save it for something bigger? What would you save up for?

Words to Know

earning: Getting money by doing a job or chore — working in exchange for payment.
income: The money you earn from doing a job or providing a service.
saving: Keeping money set aside to use later, instead of spending it right away.
spending: Using money to buy something right now.
piggy bank: A container — often shaped like a pig — used to save coins at home.

How Does Penny Help Christopher Decide What to Buy?

It was Friday and Christopher had 75 cents saved in Penny the Piggy Bank. 'I want to spend some of it!' he said. Penny gave him a knowing look. 'Before you spend,' she said wisely, 'let me teach you the most important money lesson of all: the difference between NEEDS and WANTS.'

A NEED is something you must have to live a safe and healthy life. Food, water, clothing, shelter (a home to live in), and healthcare are all needs. Without these things, you could not survive or stay healthy. Families always pay for needs first.

A WANT is something you would LIKE to have but do not absolutely need to survive. Toys, video games, candy, and fancy sneakers are wants. Wants are not bad — it is wonderful to enjoy nice things — but they come AFTER needs.

'Here is the tricky part,' said Penny. 'Money is limited. You cannot have everything, so you must CHOOSE. Every time you decide to buy one thing, you give up buying something else. That is called a trade-off.'

Christopher looked at his 75 cents. He wanted to buy a toy car (50¢), a bag of apple slices (25¢), and a sticker book (40¢). He only had 75¢. 'The apple slices are food — that is a need,' he said slowly. 'I will buy those first. Then I have 50¢ left, just enough for the toy car. I will save up more for the sticker book another week!' Penny rattled with pride. 'Now THAT is a smart money choice!'

Questions

1. What is the difference between a need and a want? Give two examples of each.
2. What is a trade-off? Give an example using money.
3. Christopher had 75¢. He spent 25¢ on apple slices and 50¢ on a toy car. How much does he have left? Show your work.
4. Why should needs always come before wants when spending money?
5. This week you learned: what money is, coin values, counting coins, earning and saving, and needs vs. wants. Which lesson was your favorite and why?
6. LET'S DISCUSS: If your family had exactly $10 left this week, what would you spend it on — and what would you leave off the list? How did you decide?

Words to Know

need: Something you must have to stay safe and healthy — food, water, clothing, shelter, healthcare.
want: Something you would like to have but do not need to survive — toys, candy, extra clothes.
limited: Not having an unlimited amount — money is limited, so you must choose how to use it.
trade-off: When you choose one thing and give up something else because you cannot have both.
choice: Deciding between two or more options — good money choices put needs before wants.

Week Summary & Teaching Notes for the Parent

This week followed a causal arc through the fundamentals of money. Monday introduced what money IS — solving the problem of bartering — and established the difference between goods and services. Tuesday focused on the four US coins (penny, nickel, dime, quarter), their values, and key equivalencies. Wednesday built on coin knowledge with counting collections of like coins using skip-counting strategies. Thursday introduced earning and saving, and practiced single-step addition within 20 using money story problems. Friday brought everything together with the needs vs. wants framework and real-world decision-making under a budget constraint.

Penny the Piggy Bank served as the narrator throughout, experiencing each concept from the perspective of a cheerful coin-collector who models good saving and spending habits.

Key concepts to check for genuine understanding — not just recall: 1) Why money was invented (bartering problems) — not just 'people use money to buy things.' 2) The DIME is the smallest coin but worth more than a penny or nickel. 3) Skip-counting rules (x1 for pennies, x5 for nickels, x10 for dimes, x25 for quarters). 4) The difference between earning (doing work) and saving (keeping for later). 5) Needs MUST come before wants when money is limited — and that trade-offs are real.

Common misconceptions to watch for: • 'Bigger coin = more valuable' — the dime disproves this; value and size are unrelated. • 'Wants are bad' — they are not! The point is priority, not prohibition. • 'Saving means hiding money' — saving is intentional and purposeful, not just setting aside.

Suggested follow-on activities: keep a simple weekly savings log; visit a grocery store and practice identifying needs vs. wants in the aisles; let Christopher earn and save toward a specific goal, tracking progress on a chart.
