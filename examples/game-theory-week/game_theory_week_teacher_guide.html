Game Theory Week — Teacher / Parent Guide

Theme: Game Theory  |  Audience: Christopher, grade 1–2  |  Narrator: Ace the Fox

Causal Arc: Win Conditions → Cooperation vs. Competition → Basic Probability → Hidden Information → Strategy

How the week works: Each day is a mixed lesson. PLAY the day's game first (every game sets up and plays in well under 45 minutes with household items), THEN read the passage and do the worksheet. The concept always comes out of a game the child has just experienced.

Monday — How Do You Win? (Win Conditions)

Games — Game Night Olympics (~20 min): Play four quick micro-games and, after each, ask "how did you win that one?" (1) Tic-Tac-Toe — 3×3 grid, three in a row (skill; can end in a tie). (2) Rock-Paper-Scissors — best of five (hidden until the reveal → foreshadows Thursday). (3) Coin-Flip Call-It — call heads/tails before the flip (pure luck → foreshadows Wednesday). (4) Race to 20 — roll a die and add up; first to 20 (luck + choices → foreshadows Friday). The point: every game, big or small, has a win condition, and this week is about learning to spot it and plan for it.

Answer Key — Reading Questions

Q1 (Win condition): The win condition is the way you win a game — the goal you must reach to be the winner.

Q2 (Tic-Tac-Toe): Get three of your own marks (three X's or three O's) in a row — across, down, or diagonally.

Q3 (Ask first): If you do not know how to win, you cannot aim your moves at the goal. Knowing the win condition lets you plan ahead instead of wasting turns.

Q4 (Tie): A tie is when the game ends with no winner and everyone is even. Yes — a game (like Tic-Tac-Toe) can end with no winner.

Matching Answer Key

Tic-Tac-Toe → Get three of your marks in a row

Rock-Paper-Scissors → Win the most throws (best of five)

Coin-Flip Call-It → Guess the coin flip correctly

Race to 20 → Be the first to reach 20 points

Hide-and-Seek → Find all of the players who are hiding

Musical Chairs → Be the last player sitting in a chair

LET'S DISCUSS Guidance

"Is there a game where you win by NOT doing something?"

Yes — lots! Freeze dance / statues (win by not moving), the quiet game (win by not talking), "don't laugh," or Jenga (you lose if the tower falls on your turn). These have a negative win condition. Great chance to notice that goals can be "do X" or "avoid X."

Misconceptions to Watch

"The winner is whoever goes first / whoever is oldest." Redirect to the actual rule: the win condition is defined by the game, not by who the player is. Also normalize the tie — a tie is a real, fair outcome, not a failure.

Extension Activity

Play Tic-Tac-Toe a few times, then ask: "Is it possible to never lose?" Show that if both players play carefully, it always ends in a tie. First peek at strategy — sets up Friday.

Tuesday — Playing With or Against (Cooperation vs. Competition)

Game — Block Tower, two rounds (~20 min): Round 1 (competition): each player builds their own tower; tallest standing tower wins. Round 2 (cooperation): build ONE tower together, as tall as possible before it topples. Contrast how the two rounds feel.

Answer Key — Reading Questions

Q1 (Competition): Playing against other players and trying to beat them; only one player or team can win. (Any example: a race, Tic-Tac-Toe.)

Q2 (Cooperation): Working together toward the same goal; everyone wins or loses together. Different from competition because you are on the same team, not against each other.

Q3 (Which round): Round 1 (each own tower) = competition. Round 2 (one shared tower) = cooperation.

Q4 (Secret vs. share): In competition, sharing your best move helps your opponent beat you, so you keep it secret. In cooperation, your teammates' success IS your success, so sharing your best idea helps everyone win.

T-Chart Answer Key

Working Together (Cooperation): Building one block tower as tall as you can; Doing a jigsaw puzzle together; Cleaning up all the toys before the timer beeps; A team of ants carrying one big crumb; Rowing a boat together.

Playing to Win (Competition): Playing Tic-Tac-Toe; A game of tag; Racing to the mailbox and back; A spelling bee; Seeing who can jump the highest.

LET'S DISCUSS Guidance

"Can a game be both?"

Yes — team games like soccer, relay races, or capture the flag: teammates COOPERATE with each other while the two teams COMPETE. This "cooperate within, compete between" idea is a big real-life theme (families, classrooms, sports).

Misconceptions to Watch

"Cooperation means there is no winner, so it's boring / not a real game." Emphasize that in cooperative games the players win TOGETHER against the challenge (the falling tower, the timer). The goal and the win are still real.

Extension Activity

Play the same simple activity (e.g., a card-flip counting game) once competitively and once cooperatively. Ask Christopher which felt better and why — there is no wrong answer; the point is noticing the difference.

Wednesday — How Likely Is It? (Basic Probability)

Game — Mystery Bag (~15 min): 5 red + 3 blue + 2 yellow objects (10 total) in an opaque bag. Christopher GUESSES the most-likely color first, then draws-tally-replace ten times. Keep the tally for the bar graph on the next page. (Replacing each draw keeps the odds steady — important for the prediction to hold.)

Answer Key — Reading Questions

Q1 (Probability): Probability is how likely something is to happen.

Q2 (Likely/unlikely): Likely: pulling RED (most marbles). Unlikely: pulling YELLOW (fewest marbles). Accept any correct pairing based on the counts.

Q3 (Prediction): Personal response. The strong prediction is red (5 of 10). Discuss whether the actual tally matched — with only 10 draws it usually leans red but may not be exact.

Q4 (Luck vs. skill): All luck: flipping a coin, rolling a die (you cannot control it). All skill: a spelling test (your effort decides). Many games mix both.

Bar Graph — What to Check

This is a MAKE-a-graph page — Christopher colors one square per tally mark, so bars will vary. Check that: (a) each bar's height matches the tally count, (b) the tallest bar is usually Red, and (c) the graph reading answers are consistent with the bars he drew. Expected shape (over many trials): Red > Blue > Yellow, mirroring 5 > 3 > 2.

Q3 (why red most): There are more red marbles than any other color, so red is the most likely to be pulled. Real results may wobble because 10 draws is a small sample.

LET'S DISCUSS Guidance

"9 red and 1 green — which will you probably pull? Could you still pull green?"

You will almost certainly pull red (very likely). But green is still POSSIBLE, just unlikely — it is not impossible. Key idea: likely does not mean certain, and unlikely does not mean impossible.

Misconceptions to Watch

"It didn't come out exactly 5-3-2, so the prediction was wrong." With only 10 draws, results wobble around the expected amounts. More draws (try 30–40) get closer to the true ratio. Also watch the gambler's idea that a color is "due" — each draw is independent.

Extension Activity

Repeat the experiment with 30 draws and tally again — the bars should line up closer to 5:3:2. Or change the bag (e.g., 8 red, 2 blue) and predict BEFORE drawing how the graph will change.

Thursday — What You Can't See (Hidden Information)

Game — Go Fish (~15 min): Deal 5–7 cards each; hold your hand hidden. Ask for ranks to make pairs; "Go Fish" when the opponent lacks the card. Point out that each player's hand is hidden information.

Answer Key — Reading Questions

Q1 (Hidden information): Anything in a game you cannot see or know. Example: your opponent's cards in Go Fish; face-down memory cards; where players hide in hide-and-seek.

Q2 (Go Fish): Your opponent's cards are hidden from you (and yours from them).

Q3 (Good players): They watch closely, remember what has happened, and use clues to make smart guesses.

Q4 (Open game): A game where you can see everything, nothing hidden — e.g., Tic-Tac-Toe or Checkers.

Frayer Model — Model Answers

What it means: Something in a game you cannot see or know.

Games that have it: Go Fish, memory/matching, hide-and-seek, Guess Who, Battleship.

What a smart player does: Pays attention, remembers clues, makes smart guesses, and hides their own information.

Draw a picture: Accept any reasonable drawing — e.g., a hand of cards held up so no one can see, or face-down cards. (This box is open-ended.)

LET'S DISCUSS Guidance

"Why would seeing your opponent's cards ruin Go Fish?"

If you could see everything, there would be no guessing — you would just take every pair instantly and the game would be over with no thinking or fun. Hidden information is what makes the game a challenge and keeps it exciting.

Misconceptions to Watch

"Hidden information means cheating / peeking is allowed." Clarify the opposite: the information is supposed to stay hidden; the skill is making good guesses WITHOUT seeing. Peeking breaks the game.

Extension Activity

Play a quick round of "20 Questions" — the answer is hidden information, and yes/no questions are clues that narrow it down. Notice how each answer removes possibilities.

Friday — Making a Plan to Win (Strategy — Capstone)

Game — "Don't Take the Last One" / Nim (~15 min): Line up 10 objects. Players alternate taking 1, 2, or 3. Whoever must take the LAST object loses. Encourage Christopher to hunt for a repeatable winning trick.

Answer Key — Reading Questions

Q1 (Strategy): A plan you make to reach your win condition — a set of steps that gives you the best chance to win.

Q2 (Strategy vs. random): A strategist thinks ahead ("if I do this, then what?") and aims moves at the goal; a random player just picks any move with no plan.

Q3 (Four ideas): Win condition, cooperation vs. competition, luck/probability, and hidden information.

Q4 (Nim strategy): Think backward: try to leave your opponent with exactly 1 object, so they must take it and lose. (Deeper trick, optional: leave a multiple of 4 — 8, then 4 — because whatever they take (1–3), you take enough to return to a multiple of 4.)

Writing Scaffold — What to Check

Open-ended — Christopher picks any game. Look for: a correctly stated win condition, a reasonable luck/skill judgment, and a strategy written as STEPS ("first..., then..."). The step-by-step form is the standard being practiced (a strategy = an algorithm). Encourage full sentences and this week's vocabulary.

LET'S DISCUSS Guidance

"Can a great strategy still lose? Can luck beat a plan?"

Yes. In games with chance (dice, cards), bad luck can beat a good plan on any single game. A good strategy improves your ODDS over many games, but does not guarantee any one win. In pure-skill games with no luck (Tic-Tac-Toe, Nim), a perfect strategy cannot be beaten by luck — only by a mistake.

Misconceptions to Watch

"A strategy guarantees a win." Reinforce that strategy improves your chances; luck and the opponent's choices still matter. Also: "strategy is just for grown-ups / hard games" — even Tic-Tac-Toe has one.

Extension Activity

Play Nim starting from different pile sizes and let Christopher discover which starting position lets the first player force a win. Or invent a brand-new game together and write down its rules, win condition, and one strategy.

Week Summary — The Building Blocks of a Good Player

The week built one idea on the next:

  1. Monday: Every game has a win condition — always ask "how do I win?" first.
  2. Tuesday: Games are competitive or cooperative (or both) — know which you are playing.
  3. Wednesday: Probability — some outcomes are more likely; use what you know to predict.
  4. Thursday: Hidden information — you cannot see everything; use clues and memory.
  5. Friday: Strategy ties it together — a step-by-step plan to reach your win condition.

By Friday, Christopher should be able to look at any game and name its win condition, say whether it is cooperative or competitive and how much is luck vs. skill, and describe a simple strategy — the habits of a thoughtful player and a thoughtful person.
