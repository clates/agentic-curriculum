Matter Week — Teacher / Parent Guide

Theme: Matter: Solids, Liquids, and Gases  |  Audience: Christopher, age 6, K–1  |  Narrator: Marco the Matter Mole

Causal Arc: What Is Matter? → Solids (properties) → Liquids (flow, containers) → Gases (invisible, fill space) → Matter Changes State (heat adds/removes energy)

Monday — What Is Matter?

Answer Key — Reading Questions

Q1 (What is matter): Matter is anything that takes up space and has mass. Accept any two objects from the room — chair, desk, crayon, window, water bottle, etc.

Q2 (Three states): Solid, liquid, and gas.

Q3 (Marco’s examples): Solid = dirt/soil he tunnels through. Liquid = puddle he splashes in. Gas = air he breathes.

LET’S DISCUSS Guidance

“Is a shadow made of matter? What about a smell?”

Neither a shadow nor a smell is matter. A shadow is the absence of light — it has no mass and takes up no space. A smell is caused by tiny particles (molecules) that float through air — the molecules themselves are matter, but the “smell” is just information detected by your nose. Encourage Christopher to argue both sides before revealing the answer.

Word Sort Answer Key

Solid: Rock, Ice cube, Pencil, Book

Liquid: Milk, Juice, Rain water, Honey

Gas: Steam, Air, Oxygen, Smoke

Note: “Ice cube” is a solid (frozen water). “Steam” is a gas (water vapor). If Christopher says ice is a liquid because it “comes from water,” that is a great teaching moment for state changes (Friday’s topic).

Misconceptions to Watch

“Only things I can hold are matter.” Air and other gases are matter even though they are invisible. Ask: “Can you feel wind? Can you blow up a balloon? Then air must be real!”

Extension Activity

Matter scavenger hunt: walk through three rooms and make a tally chart — how many solids, liquids, and gases can you find and name? Try to find at least 10 solids, 3 liquids, and 2 gases.

Tuesday — All About Solids

Answer Key — Reading Questions

Q1 (Solid vs. liquid): A solid has a definite shape that stays the same — it does not flow. A liquid changes shape to match its container.

Q2 (Four properties): Any four of: color, shape, texture (rough/smooth), size (big/small), weight (heavy/light), hardness.

Q3 (Breaking the rock): The color stays the same — both pieces are still gray. This tells us that most physical properties stay the same when you change the size of a solid.

Feature Matrix Answer Key

Rock: Hard ✓, Smooth ✓ (most pebbles), Has a fixed shape ✓

Crayon: Hard ✓, Smooth ✓, Can be held in one hand ✓, Has a fixed shape ✓

Sponge: Soft ✓, Rough/bumpy ✓ (many sponges), Can be held in one hand ✓, Has a fixed shape ✓

Coin: Hard ✓, Smooth ✓, Can be held in one hand ✓, Has a fixed shape ✓

Cotton ball: Soft ✓, Can be held in one hand ✓, Has a fixed shape ✓

Note: All five are solids, so all have “Has a fixed shape” checked. Sponge and cotton ball are soft solids — use this to address the misconception that “soft = not a solid.”

LET’S DISCUSS Guidance

“Could you make a solid into a different shape without breaking it?”

Some solids (like clay or modeling dough) can be molded into new shapes — but they are still solids because the clay holds its new shape. Ask: is clay still a solid after you squish it? (Yes!) Contrast with ice, which must MELT (change state) to become shapeless. Playdough is a great real example to explore this gray area.

Misconceptions to Watch

“Soft things are not solids.” Hardness is a property of some solids, not the definition. A sponge, a cotton ball, and a pillow are all solids because they hold their shape — you do not pour them into a container and have them change shape.

Extension Activity

Property sort: gather 8–10 small objects (coins, erasers, rocks, buttons, sponge pieces). Make a chart with columns: Color, Hard/Soft, Smooth/Rough, Small/Large. Fill in one row per object. Then sort the objects into two groups based on one chosen property — who can explain how they sorted?

Wednesday — All About Liquids

Answer Key — Reading Questions

Q1 (Liquid vs. solid): A liquid flows and takes the shape of its container; a solid keeps its own shape. The same liquid in a round bowl looks round; in a tall bottle it looks tall and thin.

Q2 (Places to find water): Any three of: rivers, ocean, puddles, our bodies, food we eat, rain, ponds, lakes, bottles, cups.

Q3 (Tilted surface): Water flows downhill to the lowest point because liquids always flow toward the lowest available space.

Cause-and-Effect Answer Key

Cause 1 → Effect: The water changes shape to fit the tall, narrow bottle — it looks long and thin instead of round.

Cause 2 → Effect: The water flows downhill to the lowest end of the tilted table.

Cause 3 → Effect: The water flows out between your fingers and drips down — liquids cannot hold their shape in an open hand.

Cause 4 → Effect: The juice takes the star shape of the bowl — it fills the container and matches its shape.

LET’S DISCUSS Guidance

“Does juice in a star-shaped container become star-shaped? Is it still the same amount?”

Yes to both! The juice takes the star shape but the amount does not change — this is conservation of volume. Pour juice between differently shaped containers to demonstrate that the volume stays constant even though the shape changes. This is a Piagetian conservation concept that 6-year-olds are just developing — be patient if Christopher says “it’s more” in the taller container.

Misconceptions to Watch

“There is more water in the tall, thin container than in the short, wide one.” This is a classic conservation-of-volume misconception. Demonstrate by pouring back and forth between containers, confirming the amount is the same. The shape changed; the amount did not.

Extension Activity

Container experiment: find 4–5 different-shaped containers (cup, bowl, jar, pitcher). Pour the same measured amount of water into each. Observe how the water looks different in each container. Confirm the same amount by pouring each back into a measuring cup.

Thursday — All About Gases

Answer Key — Reading Questions

Q1 (Gas properties): A gas has no fixed shape and no fixed size; it spreads out to fill all the space in its container. Contrast: solid = fixed shape; liquid = fixed volume but takes container shape; gas = no fixed shape or volume.

Q2 (Evidence for air): Any two of: feel wind when it moves; blow bubbles in water; inflate a ball or tire; pinwheel spins; feel it on skin when you blow; sound travels through it.

Q3 (Three gases named): Water vapor (steam), carbon dioxide, helium.

Odd One Out Answer Key

Row 1: Air — the others (Rock, Marble, Wood block) are solids; Air is a gas.

Row 2: Pebble — the others (Water, Milk, Juice) are liquids; Pebble is a solid.

Row 3: Honey — the others (Steam, Carbon dioxide, Oxygen) are gases; Honey is a liquid.

Row 4: Ice cube — the others (Balloon air, Helium, Water vapor) are gases; Ice cube is a solid.

Row 5: “Keeps its own shape” — this describes a solid; the others (Flows easily, Can be poured, Takes shape of container) describe liquids or gases.

LET’S DISCUSS Guidance

“When you pop a balloon, where does the gas go?”

The gas spreads out into the room. It does not disappear — it just mixes with the rest of the air in the room. Gases always expand to fill their container. The “container” is now the room. Ask: “Could you catch it again?” (Not easily, because it is now mixed with all the room air — but it is still there!)

Misconceptions to Watch

“Steam is a gas you can see.” The visible white cloud above a boiling pot is actually tiny liquid water droplets — condensed water vapor. True water vapor (gas) is completely invisible. The gas becomes visible only when it has cooled enough to condense into droplets. This is subtle — just plant the seed.

Extension Activity

Gas in a bottle: put a balloon over the mouth of an empty plastic bottle. Squeeze the bottle — the balloon inflates. Release — it deflates. What is inflating the balloon? (Air — a gas — that was in the bottle.) This proves air is real matter that can be pushed and moves around.

Friday — Matter Changes State (Capstone)

Answer Key — Reading Questions

Q1 (Solid + heat): When you add heat to a solid it melts and becomes a liquid. This change is called melting.

Q2 (Liquid − heat): When you take heat away from a liquid it freezes and becomes a solid. Real-life example: water in the freezer becomes ice.

Q3 (Ice/water/steam): Ice, water, and steam are the same material (water). What is different is the state — which depends on the amount of heat. Ice is cold (solid), liquid water is at room/warm temperature, steam is very hot (gas).

Q4 (Ice → steam chain): Ice (solid, cold) → add heat → melts to liquid water → add more heat → evaporates to steam/water vapor (gas).

Tree Map Answer Key

Solid (4): Rock, Ice cube, Wood, Crayon

Liquid (4): Water, Juice, Milk, Honey

Gas (4): Air, Steam, Oxygen, Carbon dioxide

LET’S DISCUSS Guidance

“When ice melts in your juice, does the matter disappear?”

No! The ice becomes liquid water and mixes with the juice. The matter is still there — it just changed state (solid ice → liquid water). The juice gets watery because more liquid (the melted ice) was added. This is a great conservation-of-matter moment. Ask Christopher: “Where did the ice go?” Accept “it turned into water” as the key insight.

Misconceptions to Watch

“The ice disappeared.” Matter cannot disappear — it only changes form. Melted ice becomes liquid water. Evaporated water becomes invisible gas. Reinforce: we cannot make matter appear from nothing or make it vanish. This is the law of conservation of matter (simplified for K–1 as “matter does not disappear”).

Extension Activity

Three-state water exploration: (1) observe an ice cube (solid); (2) let it melt into liquid water; (3) with supervision, heat a small amount of water in a pot and watch steam rise. At each stage, ask: “What state is it now? What changed?” Draw a simple diagram showing ice → water → steam with arrows labeled “add heat.”

Week Summary — Causal Chain

The week followed this chain of ideas:

  1. Monday: Everything is matter (has mass and takes up space); matter comes in three states: solid, liquid, gas.
  2. Tuesday: Solids have a definite shape and can be described by physical properties (color, shape, texture, size, weight).
  3. Wednesday: Liquids have no fixed shape; they flow and take the shape of their container; water flows downhill.
  4. Thursday: Gases have no fixed shape or size; they spread out to fill all available space; air is an invisible but real gas.
  5. Friday: Adding or removing heat causes matter to change state: melt (solid → liquid), freeze (liquid → solid), evaporate (liquid → gas), condense (gas → liquid).

By Friday, Christopher should be able to name all three states of matter, give a real example of each, and explain what happens to ice when it is heated and what happens to water when it is put in the freezer — connecting the change to heat energy.
