Meet Marco — Everything Is Matter!

Meet Marco the Mole! Marco has velvety dark fur, tiny pink paws, and a twitchy nose that can smell things underground. Every day, Marco tunnels through the soil, swims through puddles, and breathes in the fresh air — and this week he is going to teach us something amazing: EVERYTHING around us is made of matter!

Matter is anything that takes up space and has mass. Mass means it has some weight — you can feel it if you pick it up. Look around the room. The chair you sit on is matter. The water you drink is matter. Even the air you breathe is matter! If you can touch it, feel it, smell it, or measure it, it is matter.

Scientists sort matter into three groups called states of matter. The three states are: solid, liquid, and gas. Solids have a shape that stays the same unless you break or cut them. Liquids flow and take the shape of whatever container you put them in. Gases spread out to fill all the space they are in.

Marco knows all three states from his daily adventures. 'The dirt I tunnel through is a solid,' he says. 'The puddle I splash in is a liquid. And the air I breathe is a gas!' This week we will explore each state of matter with Marco — and discover how matter can even change from one state to another!

Questions

1. What is matter? Name two things in the room that are made of matter.
2. What are the three states of matter? List all three.
3. Marco experiences all three states of matter every day. What solid, liquid, and gas does he encounter?
4. LET'S DISCUSS: Is a shadow made of matter? What about a smell? How can you tell if something is matter or not?

Words to Know

matter: Anything that takes up space and has mass — everything around us is made of matter.
mass: How much material is in an object — things with mass have weight when you pick them up.
solid: A state of matter with a shape that stays the same, like a rock or a crayon.
liquid: A state of matter that flows and takes the shape of its container, like water or juice.
gas: A state of matter that spreads out to fill all the space it is in, like air.

Marco Explores the Underground — Solids Have Their Own Shape!

Marco was digging his tunnel when he hit something hard. 'A pebble!' he squeaked. He held it in his paw and noticed it was round, smooth, and gray. He set it down and it kept its shape — it did not spread out or flow away. 'Solids are amazing,' Marco thought. 'They always keep their own shape!'

A solid is a state of matter with a definite shape and a definite size. 'Definite' means it stays the same. If you pick up a rock and put it in a jar, the rock is still the same shape — the rock does not become jar-shaped. Solids do not flow like water. You cannot pour a crayon into a cup!

We can describe solids using their physical properties. Physical properties are things you can observe with your senses. Color is a property — the pebble is gray. Shape is a property — the pebble is round. Texture is a property — the pebble is smooth. Size and weight are properties — a pebble is small and light, while a boulder is large and heavy.

Even though you can change a solid by cutting it or breaking it, most properties stay the same. If you break a gray rock in half, both pieces are still gray! The color did not change. Marco discovered that knowing a solid's properties helps him figure out what it is and how to use it in his underground home.

Questions

1. What makes a solid different from a liquid? Use the word 'shape' in your answer.
2. Name four physical properties you can use to describe a solid.
3. If you break a gray rock in half, what happens to its color? What does this tell us?
4. LET'S DISCUSS: Could you make a solid into a different shape without breaking it? Can you think of any solid that changes shape easily — and is it still a solid?

Words to Know

solid: A state of matter with a definite (fixed) shape and size — it does not flow.
physical property: Something you can observe about matter using your senses — color, shape, texture, or size.
texture: How something feels when you touch it — rough, smooth, bumpy, soft, or hard.
definite: Fixed and not changing — a solid has a definite shape that stays the same.

Marco Finds a Puddle — Liquids Flow and Change Shape!

One morning, Marco crawled out of his tunnel and found a big rain puddle. He splashed his paw in — the water rippled and moved. He picked up a pawful of water and it dripped right through his claws. 'Liquids do not hold their shape!' he noticed. 'They flow wherever there is space!'

A liquid is a state of matter that flows and takes the shape of whatever container it is in. Water in a round bowl looks round. The same water poured into a tall, thin bottle looks long and thin. The amount of water does not change — but its shape does, because liquids flow. This is very different from a solid, which keeps its shape no matter what container it is in.

Water is the most important liquid on Earth. Water has many special properties. It is clear and colorless when pure. It has no smell. It can be poured, splashed, and dripped. Water flows downhill — if you spill water on a tilted table, it runs to the lowest point. Water can also be found in many places: in rivers, in the ocean, in puddles, in our bodies, and even in the food we eat!

'Water is everywhere,' Marco said happily, shaking the droplets from his fur. 'And it is always the same water — just in different containers and places.'

Questions

1. How is a liquid different from a solid? What happens to a liquid's shape in different containers?
2. Name three places you can find water as a liquid.
3. What happens to water when it is on a tilted surface? Why?
4. LET'S DISCUSS: If you pour juice into a container shaped like a star, does the juice become star-shaped? Is the juice still the same amount? What does this tell you about liquids?

Words to Know

liquid: A state of matter that flows and takes the shape of whatever container it is in.
flow: To move smoothly from place to place — liquids flow; solids do not.
container: An object that holds something inside it — a cup, a bowl, or a bottle.
property: A describing word for matter — like color, smell, texture, or whether something flows.

Marco Breathes Deep — Gases Fill Every Space!

Marco climbed up out of the ground and took a big, deep breath. He could not see the air, but he could feel it on his fur and smell the flowers in it. 'Air is a gas,' he thought. 'And even though I cannot see it, it is definitely matter!'

A gas is a state of matter that has no fixed shape and no fixed size. Gases spread out to fill ALL of the space inside any container. If you blow air into a balloon, the air spreads out to fill every corner of the balloon. If you open the balloon, the air spreads out into the whole room! Gases will always fill their container completely.

Air is the gas we know best. Air is all around us — we cannot see it, but we know it is there because we can feel the wind when it moves, we can blow bubbles in water with it, and we can inflate a ball or a tire with it. Air is actually a mixture of many gases: mostly nitrogen, some oxygen (the part we breathe), and small amounts of other gases.

Other gases you might know: steam that comes off hot food is water vapor (water as a gas). Carbon dioxide is the gas you breathe OUT. Helium is the gas that makes balloons float. All of these gases are invisible, but they are real matter — they take up space! Marco puffed out his cheeks with a big mouthful of air. 'I have a gas inside me right now,' he laughed.

Questions

1. What makes a gas different from a solid and a liquid? What does a gas do when put in a container?
2. Even though you cannot see air, how do you know it is there? Give two pieces of evidence.
3. Name three gases besides air that are mentioned in the passage.
4. LET'S DISCUSS: If a gas always fills its container completely — what happens to the gas when you open a jar or pop a balloon? Where does the gas go?

Words to Know

gas: A state of matter with no fixed shape or size — it spreads out to fill all available space.
air: The mixture of gases all around us — mostly nitrogen and oxygen.
water vapor: Water in its gas form — the invisible steam that rises from hot water.
carbon dioxide: A gas you breathe out after your body uses oxygen.
invisible: Cannot be seen — gases are usually invisible, but they are still real matter.

Marco's Big Experiment — Heating and Cooling Change Matter!

Marco found an ice cube on the kitchen counter and watched it carefully. First it was a solid — cold and hard with a definite shape. Then, as the room warmed it up, it started to change. 'It is melting!' Marco squeaked. The solid ice was becoming a liquid puddle!

Matter can change from one state to another when you add heat or take heat away. When you add heat to a solid, it can MELT and become a liquid. When water is heated even more — to very high temperatures — it can EVAPORATE and become a gas called water vapor. You can see this as steam rising from a hot pot on the stove.

Cooling does the opposite! When you take heat away from a liquid, it can FREEZE and become a solid. Water in a freezer turns to ice because the freezer takes heat away. When water vapor in the air cools down, it can CONDENSE and become liquid water — this is how morning dew forms on grass!

The most amazing thing is that the matter is still the same. Water, ice, and steam are all made of the exact same material — just in different states! Only the amount of heat changes which state the water is in. Marco stared at the tiny puddle where the ice cube used to be. 'It did not disappear,' he said with wonder. 'It just changed state!'

Questions

1. What happens when you add heat to a solid? What is this change called?
2. What happens when you take heat away from a liquid? Give a real-life example.
3. Ice, water, and steam are all the same material. What is different about them?
4. Describe the full chain of changes you could make to a single ice cube using only heat: start with ice and end with steam.
5. LET'S DISCUSS: When ice melts in your juice and the juice gets watery, is that the same kind of change? Does the matter disappear, or does it just become something else?

Words to Know

melt: When a solid is heated and changes into a liquid — like ice melting into water.
freeze: When a liquid is cooled and changes into a solid — like water turning into ice.
evaporate: When a liquid is heated and changes into a gas — like water turning into steam.
condense: When a gas cools down and changes into a liquid — like steam turning back into water drops.
state of matter: One of the three forms matter can take: solid, liquid, or gas.
heat: Energy that causes matter to warm up — adding or removing heat changes the state of matter.

Week Summary & Teaching Notes for the Parent

This week followed a causal arc through the states of matter. Monday introduced matter itself — the idea that everything has mass and takes up space — and met Marco the Mole, our narrator. Tuesday explored solids and their physical properties (color, shape, texture, size, weight), with the key idea that solids keep a definite shape. Wednesday explored liquids and how they flow to fill any container, using water as the central example. Thursday explored gases — that they are invisible matter that expands to fill all available space, with air as the primary example. Friday pulled everything together with state changes: melting, freezing, evaporation, and condensation show that the same matter can exist in different states depending on temperature.

Marco the Matter Mole appeared throughout the week, tunneling through solids, splashing in liquids, and breathing gases — giving Christopher a concrete, living-world perspective on abstract science concepts.

Key concepts to check for genuine understanding — not just recall: 1) Matter is anything with mass that takes up space — including invisible things like air. 2) Solids keep their shape; liquids take the shape of their container; gases fill all space. 3) Physical properties (color, shape, texture, size) let us describe and sort solids. 4) Water is the same material whether it is ice, liquid water, or steam — only state changes. 5) Heating adds energy that causes state changes; cooling removes energy.

Common misconceptions to watch for: • 'Gases are not matter' — air and other gases are real matter; they have mass (blow up a ball and it gets heavier). • 'Ice disappears when it melts' — matter is conserved; the liquid water is still there. • 'Steam is visible' — visible steam is actually tiny liquid droplets; true water vapor is invisible. • 'Soft things are not solids' — sponges and cotton balls are solids; hardness is one property, not the definition of a solid.

Suggested follow-on activities: explore non-Newtonian fluids (cornstarch + water) — is oobleck a solid or a liquid? Make ice pops and observe all three states of water. Find five solids, five liquids, and five gases around the house together.
